Inside a Metal

A metal is a grid of positive ions sitting in a "sea" of electrons that roam freely between them. That one idea explains why metals hold together, conduct electricity and bend instead of shattering.

What the electron sea explains
PropertyWhy
StrongEvery positive ion is pulled on by the negative electrons all around it. Lots of attraction in every direction.
High melting pointIt takes a lot of heat energy to overcome all that attraction.
Conducts electricityThe electrons are free to move, so they can flow through the metal when it's connected to a battery.
Conducts heatMoving electrons carry energy quickly from the hot end to the cold end.
MalleableLayers of ions can slide over each other. The electron sea moves with them, so the bonds aren't broken.

Compare: three ways atoms hold together

Metallic bonding

Positive metal ions in a sea of delocalised electrons that belong to no single atom. Example: copper.

Ionic bonding

Positive and negative ions electrostatically attracted in a fixed grid. Electrons have been handed over, not shared. Example: sodium chloride. See the Precipitate Lab.

Covalent bonding

Non-metal atoms share pairs of electrons. The shared electrons stay between those two atoms. Examples: the gases and water below.

The small molecules on your sheet

Unlike in a metal, the electrons here stay put: each pair of dots is two electrons shared between two atoms. A lone hydrogen or oxygen atom isn't stable, so they pair up. That's why the gases are written H2 and O2.
